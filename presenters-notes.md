## The 2 Slices

### Why was the adjacency-based indirect effects rule replaced?


The adjacency-based indirect effects rule (originally proposed by Alex) was replaced because it was **inefficient, economically counterproductive, and conceptually misaligned** with how prefabricated construction costs actually work. 

Specifically, the Software Engineering Lead replaced the rule for four main reasons:

#### 1. It Created Massive Over-Flagging (Worse Than the Status Quo)
The original rule dictated that any panel sharing an physical edge with a modified panel should be flagged for review. However, when tested against actual AUAR build data (revisions r3 to r4), **the adjacency rule flagged 13 out of 20 panels** (every single panel on Level 1). 
AUAR’s core business challenge is that ~12% of panels are currently remade unnecessarily. A tool that flags 65% of a build as "affected" is worse than the status quo; it would likely be ignored by busy production managers, defeating the purpose of building the tool in the first place.

#### 2. Over-Flagging Was the Failure Mode, Not a Safety Net
Alex argued that over-flagging was "the safe direction to be wrong in". While that is the correct mindset for safety-critical hardware systems, the Software Engineering Lead pointed out a fundamental **reasoning error**: the panel revisions tool is a *cost-reduction* tool. In this context, over-flagging *is* the failure mode. If a production manager blindly acted on all 13 flags generated by the adjacency rule, it would trigger **£2,340** in remake charges instead of the actual needed changes.

#### 3. Physical Proximity Does Not Determine Cost Impact
Adjacency assumes that physical closeness in a building dictates design and manufacturing impact, which is a fallacy. A panel’s actual design-change impact and cost are determined by its **manufacturing status and the type of change**, not where it sits physically:
* A **planned** panel costs **£0** to change, regardless of how many adjacent panels move, because the software simply re-queues the new specification.
* An **installed** panel requires a manual engineering **escalation and a site visit**, whether it is isolated or adjacent to a dozen changes.
Therefore, the impact classification must be a pure function of `(changeKind, status)` alone.

#### 4. It Was Replaced by a Highly Targeted Stacking Check
Instead of loose physical adjacency, the team introduced **PR-7: Geometric Consistency** (a stacking check). This check specifically looks for unchanged panels that physically no longer fit changed panels—for example, a Level 2 wall that fails to stack on a Level 1 wall that has shrunk in width. 

When run on the actual r3 → r4 fixture, the geometric consistency check flagged **exactly one real defect** (an unchanged Level 2 wall, EW-L2-W1, that was left hanging because the Level 1 wall underneath it shrank from 4800mm to 4200mm). The adjacency rule found this same defect, but buried it under 12 false positives.

## Why I picked the 2 slices I did

The Software Engineering Lead chose to work on **two slices** instead of one to resolve both a critical path project bottleneck and a fundamental architectural disagreement within the team. Although the initial handover brief only requested a single build slice, the Lead delivered two to immediately de-risk the delivery plan on Day 1.

Here is why those two specific slices were chosen:

#### Slice 1: E-Stop Audit and Stop-Response Measurement (Python)
This slice focuses on closing site inspection actions **a2** (logging and retrieving emergency stop events) and **a3** (measuring and recording response times). 

The Lead picked this slice because:
* **It is on the critical critical path:** The cell cannot legally run **unsupervised** (the core goal of the customer demo) until these site inspection actions are closed, re-tested, and signed off by an external site inspector. The inspection process takes 24 working days of strictly sequential tasks and carries a 10-working-day lead time.
* **It was unowned:** In the project handover, action **a3** had no owner and was marked as "Not agreed" because it sat in the messy grey area between software and robotics. 
* **It asserts technical leadership:** It sits directly on the boundary between software and robotics, which is exactly where a Software Engineering Lead should operate. Taking ownership converted a nagging open question into active work-in-progress on Day 1, establishing credibility with the CTO.
* **It demonstrates mathematical honesty:** The source data is limited to whole-second resolution, meaning a sub-second response cannot be precisely calculated. This slice demonstrates how to build a safety-critical audit log that honestly discloses these limitations and reports conservative uncertainty bounds (e.g., `< 2 seconds`) rather than fabricating false precision to an inspector.

#### Slice 2: Panel Revision Impact and Cost Classification (TypeScript)
This slice focuses on classifying the geometric and financial impact of design changes between build revisions (specifically testing it using the `build-r3` to `build-r4` fixture data).

The Lead picked this slice because:
* **It targets the core business value:** The entire business case for the "Panel Revisions" feature is to reduce material waste (the average £430 wasted per changed build). However, the handover briefs mistakenly marked the cost-surfacing story (PR-5) as a "Should" prioritize. This slice promotes cost classification to a "Must" to ensure the tool actually saves money instead of just generating a nicer list of "affected" panels.
* **It settles an architectural dispute in code:** The team’s Solver Lead (Alex) and Platform Lead (Jo) were on a collision course. Alex planned to build the revision history directly on top of `PanelSpec` by adding `revision` and `previousSpec` fields to it. Meanwhile, Jo was planning to completely delete `PanelSpec` in week 3 as part of a major `BuildModel` migration.
* **It proves a cleaner design is possible:** By building this slice as a pure function (`classifyRevision(previous, next)`) that takes two complete build documents in memory, the Lead proved that the code does not need a `previousSpec` field. This decoupled the revisions logic from the schema migration, saved Jo's capacity, and settled the team's dispute with a working, tested prototype rather than a meeting.

## The four supporting artefacts

### `firmware-event-schema-v4.2.md`

The artefact missing from the hand-over. v4.2 is specced as behaviour — "expose
run events, accept remote stop" — with no data contract, so nobody has written
down what the controller must *emit*.

It specifies the envelope every event needs (`eventId`, `seq`, `bootId`,
millisecond `ts`, `monotonicUs`, `class`), the delivery protocol
(at-least-once plus idempotent receive, with the server acknowledging highest
contiguous `seq`), buffer sizing, and an overflow policy that never drops a
safety event.

**The point to make:** three separate blockers — FM-4, a2's audit log, a3's
timing — turn out to be one missing document. It has to be agreed before boards
land in week three, because there's no v4.3 before the demo.

**Be precise if pushed:** it fully solves FM-4, improves a2, and only partly
addresses a3. I said "fixes all three" at first and that was too tidy.
